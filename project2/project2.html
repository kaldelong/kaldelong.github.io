ASTRAGALS

Astragals, also known as knucklebones, are the oldest known form of dice and have a history as game pieces dating back to the Neolithic Era (10000 BCE). These items were the ankle bones of hoofed animals such as goats and sheep. They had four distinct sides: the hole, the belly, the ear, and the vulture. Astragalomancy was performed in Ancient Greece through the rolling of astragals and subsequent consultation of "dice oracles," tables of divination results carved into statues or monoliths. Beyond gaming and divination, astragals also held cultural and symbolic significance, often buried with the dead or used in rituals. Their irregular shape introduced an element of chance that fascinated early societies, blending play, prophecy, and spiritual meaning in a single, enduring object.

Over time, astragals transitioned from animal bones to imitations made from other materials like stone, crystal, wood, ivory, metal, or glass. This shift was due to various reasons, including uniformity, aesthetics, and availability. Astragals were prized possessions, and are often unearthed in burial sites.

THROW STICKS

Throw Sticks were used by Ancient Egyptians, Ancient Greeks, and Indigenous Americans such as the Hopi, Tarahumara, Opata, Tepehuan, and Zaque tribes. These items had only two outcomes. The origin of these items is unknown, but throw stick artifacts have dated prior to 3000 BCE.


The most well documented throwing sticks were used as counters, like dice, in the Ancient Egyptian game of Senet. These sticks were made of wood, ivory, or other materials. They consisted of an elongated shape and two flattened sides, with one of the sides being marked or carved. Often the ends were also carved in the shape of some animal or some human parts like fingers.

The earliest known cubic dice artifacts were found in the Indus Valley (which encompasses much of present-day Iraq, parts of Afghanistan, Pakistan and northern India), and dated to 3000 BCE. The transition to cubic dice allowed for further standardization of the tool, and the six

sides increased randomness and allowed more options. Cubic dice are found in a wide range of archaeological contexts, including generalized garbage, domestic, and mortuary contexts.

The material of cubic dice varied, but the shape and number style remained fairly consistent. Most used pips (dots) to number the faces. The dot-in-circle motif recalls designs presumed to be of magical significance, most likely an abstract eye to ward off the evil-eye.

EARLY POLYHEDRAL DICE

Many polyhedral dice are often associated with modern games like Dungeons and Dragons, but they also have ancient origins. The earliest known d20 dice dates back to Egypt between the 2ndC BCE and the 4thC CE. Most often each face of the die is inscribed with a number in Greek and/or Latin, but one d20 with Egyptian god names in Demotic (the Egyptian script of these late periods) was found in the Dakhleh Oasis. This dice was likely used for divination.

Elongated dice have been common since around 2500 BCE, likely influenced by the shape of the bones used in their construction. Most were marked on the four long faces. When marked on six, their elongated shape made them more likely to fall on certain faces than others. As dice falls were often considered signs of supernatural favor or of a player’s fortune, the uneven nature was unimportant.

Teetotums are a form of top having usually 4, 6, 8, or 12 sides marked with distinctive symbols. Usage goes back to (at least) the Ancient Greeks and Romans as early as 800 BCE. The teetotum has variants such as the pirinola, used in Latin America, and the dreidel, used in Jewish communities during the holiday of Hanukkah.

MODERN DICE

Modern dice reflect a rich evolution in both form and material. Today, dice are made from a wide variety of materials, including plastic, metal, resin, stone, and even glass, each offering different tactile and aesthetic experiences. Precision-machined metal dice provide weight and balance prized by collectors and players alike, while artisanal resin or gemstone dice highlight craftsmanship and individuality.

The variety in shapes—such as d4s, d20s, and percentile dice—mirrors the complexity of contemporary games and the specialized roles dice now play. In this way, modern dice are not just tools for generating randomness; they are expressions of identity, design innovation, and the enduring human fascination with chance.
